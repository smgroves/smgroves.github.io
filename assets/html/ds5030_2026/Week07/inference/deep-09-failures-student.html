Three things that can break

Every interval is \(\hat\theta \pm c\cdot\widehat{\text{se}}\), and every test compares \(\hat\theta/\widehat{\text{se}}\) to \(c\). A failure breaks one of three things: the SE is wrong (dependence, unequal variance, no finite variance), the critical value \(c\) is wrong (small samples, skew, many tests, peeking), or the result is read wrong (coverage read as belief, significance read as importance). This page puts the first two kinds in widgets.

Dependence

\(V(\bar X) = \sigma^2/n\) used independence to drop every covariance term. With positive correlation those terms are positive, and \(s/\sqrt n\) is too small. For a series where each value is \(\rho\) times the previous plus fresh noise (AR(1)), the variance of the mean is roughly \(\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\cdot\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}\) for large \(n\).

Correlated data, independent-data formula

Top: one simulated series of length \(n\) with lag-1 correlation \(\rho\). Bottom readouts: from 3,000 series, the true SD of the mean, the average \(s/\sqrt n\), and the coverage of "\(\bar x \pm 1.96\,s/\sqrt n\)."

Work it by hand · the effective sample size

With \(\rho = 0.7\): \((1 + 0.7)/(1 - 0.7) = 5.67\). So the real variance of the mean is 5.67 times what \(s/\sqrt n\) assumes, and the real SE is \(\sqrt{5.67} = 2.4\) times larger. 100 correlated observations carry about as much information as \(100/5.67 \approx 18\) independent ones. A "95%" interval built with the naive SE is really \(\pm 1.96/2.4 = \pm 0.82\) true SEs, covering about 59%. (The course simulation: true SD 0.33, naive 0.14, coverage 59%.)

Work it by hand · equicorrelated pairs

Twins: 50 pairs, each pair's two measurements correlated 0.5. \(V(\bar X) = \frac{\sigma^2}{100}(1 + \rho) = 1.5\,\sigma^2/100\). The naive SE is too small by a factor \(\sqrt{1.5} = 1.22\). Treating 100 correlated people as 100 independent ones overstates the evidence; the effective sample size is 67.

Peeking

If you test after every batch of data and stop the first time \(p < 0.05\), the overall false-positive rate climbs with every look (CASI §3.3).

How many looks before you're fooled?

Data with no real effect. You check a z-test after each of \(k\) equally spaced looks and stop at the first "significant" one. Bars: the share of 20,000 null studies that ever declare significance.

Work it by hand · two looks (CASI's example)

One-sided at 1.645, looks at months 20 and 30. If the looks were independent, the false-positive rate would be \(1 - 0.95^2 = 0.0975\). They're positively correlated (month 30 includes months 1–20, correlation \(\sqrt{20/30} = 0.82\)), so it's less: 0.074. Still half again over the promised 0.05.

Heavy tails

When the SE doesn't exist

Running mean and running \(s/\sqrt n\) for data from a t-distribution with \(k\) degrees of freedom. For \(k \le 2\) the variance is infinite (and for \(k = 1\), the Cauchy, the mean doesn't exist): \(s/\sqrt n\) doesn't settle, and occasional huge values yank the mean.

The rest of the catalog, worked

Work it by hand · SD reported as SE

"Average price $8.59k ± $8.17k" (SD) vs "± $0.85k" (SE). The first says individual cars vary; the second says the average is pinned down. As a 95% statement about the mean, the first is 9.6 times too wide.

Work it by hand · the winner's curse (CASI §3.3)

10 estimates, each \(N(\mu_i, 1)\), all with true \(\mu_i = 0\). The largest of 10 standard normals averages about 1.54. Report "our best result was 1.5 SEs above zero" and you've reported noise: selecting the max biases it upward. CASI's prostate example: the top gene's 5.29 shrinks to 4.11 under empirical Bayes.

Work it by hand · significant but trivial

\(n = 1{,}000{,}000\), \(s = 10\): \(\widehat{\text{se}} = 0.01\). An effect of 0.03 has \(z = 3\), \(p = 0.003\), and a 95% interval of \((0.01, 0.05)\): very significant and, on a scale with SD 10, practically nothing. The interval shows that; the p-value hides it.

Work it by hand · large p, low power

\(n = 9\), \(s = 6\), \(\widehat{\text{se}} = 2\). A true effect of 2 is 1 SE: power about 0.17 (page 6). So \(p = 0.4\) here is what you'd see most of the time even if the effect is real. The 95% interval, about \(\pm 4.6\), says so honestly.

Questions

Think about itIn the dependence widget, set \(\rho\) negative. What happens to the naive interval's coverage, and why?

It goes above 95%: with negative correlation, consecutive values tend to cancel, the true variance of the mean is smaller than \(\sigma^2/n\), and the naive SE is too large. Rare in practice, but it shows the formula's error has a sign set by the correlation.

Think about itWith 10 looks at 1.96 each time, the false-positive rate is about 19%, not 50%. Why not \(1 - 0.95^{10} = 40\%\)?

The looks are positively correlated: each later test includes all the earlier data. If look 3 wasn't significant, look 4 shares most of its data and probably isn't either. Correlation makes repeated chances less independent, so the inflation is real but smaller than for independent tests.

Check your understandingFor t-distributed data with \(k = 3\), the variance is finite (it's \(k/(k-2) = 3\)). Why does the running SE still jump around?

The variance is finite but the fourth moment isn't (for \(k \le 4\)), so \(s^2\) itself has infinite variance and converges very slowly. The CLT for the mean still holds, but SE estimates are unreliable at practical sample sizes. "Finite variance" is the minimum for the theory, not a guarantee of good behavior.
