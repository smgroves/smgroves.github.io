Three kinds of "getting close"

AoS Ch 5 uses three notions of convergence. The distinction matters because the LLN is a statement about values (convergence in probability) and the CLT is a statement about histograms (convergence in distribution).

NameMeansUsed for
in quadratic mean\(E(X_n - X)^2 \to 0\)proving consistency via MSE → 0
in probability\(P(|X_n - X| > \epsilon) \to 0\) for every \(\epsilon\)LLN, consistency
in distribution\(P(X_n \le t) \to P(X \le t)\) at continuity pointsCLT, approximate intervals

Quadratic mean ⇒ probability ⇒ distribution (AoS Thm 5.4). None of the reverse arrows hold.

Work it by hand · a sequence that converges in probability but not in mean square

Let \(Y_n = n\) with probability \(1/n\), else 0.

  • \(P(|Y_n| > \epsilon) = 1/n \to 0\): \(Y_n \to 0\) in probability.
  • \(E[Y_n] = n \cdot \frac1n = 1\) for every \(n\), and \(E[Y_n^2] = n \to \infty\): no convergence in mean square.

The variable is almost always 0, but a rare enormous value keeps its mean at 1 forever. Heavy-tailed data behave like this, which is why the Cauchy distribution breaks averages.

Work it by hand · convergence in distribution without convergence in probability

Let \(X \sim N(0,1)\) and \(X_n = -X\) for every \(n\). Each \(X_n\) is also \(N(0,1)\), so \(X_n \rightsquigarrow X\) trivially. But \(|X_n - X| = 2|X|\) never shrinks. Same histogram, different values: that's all the CLT promises.

The law of large numbers, watched

Running averages

Each line is one person's running average \(\bar X_n\) as they keep sampling. The shaded band is \(\mu \pm 2\sigma/\sqrt n\). For the Cauchy, there is no \(\sigma\) and no band: watch what happens.

The x-axis is the number of draws on a log scale, from 1 to 5,000.

Formal · AoS Thm 5.6 (WLLN)

If \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) are iid with \(E|X_1| < \infty\), then \(\bar X_n \xrightarrow{P} \mu\). With finite variance the proof is Chebyshev: \(P(|\bar X_n - \mu| > \epsilon) \le \sigma^2/(n\epsilon^2)\).

Work it by hand · Chebyshev versus reality

Fair die, \(\sigma^2 = 35/12 = 2.92\), \(n = 100\), \(\epsilon = 0.5\). Chebyshev: \(P(|\bar X - 3.5| > 0.5) \le 2.92/25 = 0.117\). The CLT: \(\text{se} = 0.171\), so \(0.5/0.171 = 2.93\) SEs and \(P \approx 2\Phi(-2.93) = 0.003\). Chebyshev is about 35 times too pessimistic here. It's a guarantee for every distribution, so it has to cover the worst one.

Why the variance of a mean is σ²/n

Story proof · the random walk of errors

Each observation is truth plus an error. The sum of \(n\) independent errors is a random walk: steps that agree pile up, steps that disagree cancel, and independent steps agree and disagree equally often. After \(n\) steps of typical size \(\sigma\), the walk is typically about \(\sigma\sqrt n\) from home (not \(\sigma n\), which is what you'd get if every step agreed). Divide the sum by \(n\) and the typical error of the mean is \(\sigma\sqrt n/n = \sigma/\sqrt n\).

Work it by hand · the algebra, with covariances shown $$V\Big(\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\Big) = \sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n \text{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = \underbrace{n\sigma^2}_{i = j} + \underbrace{\sum_{i\ne j}\text{Cov}(X_i, X_j)}_{=\,0 \text{ if independent}}$$

So \(V(\bar X) = n\sigma^2/n^2 = \sigma^2/n\). Write out the \(3\times 3\) grid of covariances for \(n = 3\): three diagonal cells of \(\sigma^2\) and six off-diagonal cells that independence sets to zero. If every pair had correlation \(\rho\), the six cells would each be \(\rho\sigma^2\) and \(V(\bar X) = \frac{\sigma^2}{3}(1 + 2\rho)\). With \(\rho = 0.5\), that's double. This grid is the whole dependence failure in one picture.

The central limit theorem, built

Standardized averages

4,000 samples of size \(n\) from the chosen population. The histogram shows \(Z = \sqrt n(\bar X - \mu)/\sigma\); the curve is \(N(0,1)\). The readouts show how far the shape is from normal.

Formal · AoS Thms 5.8, 5.10, 5.11

iid, mean \(\mu\), variance \(\sigma^2 < \infty\): \(\sqrt n(\bar X_n - \mu)/\sigma \rightsquigarrow N(0,1)\), and also with \(S_n\) for \(\sigma\). Berry–Esseen: the largest gap between the CDF of the standardized mean and \(\Phi\) is at most a constant times \(E|X - \mu|^3/(\sigma^3\sqrt n)\).

Story proof · repeated smearing

The distribution of a sum of independent pieces is built by convolution: smear the current distribution by the shape of the next piece. Each smear averages away fine detail. After many smears only two numbers survive, the total center and the total spread, and the one shape that reproduces itself under smearing (normal plus normal is normal) is where everything ends up. Try "two bumps" with \(n = 1, 2, 3, 10\) in the widget: the bumps merge, then the hump rounds into a bell.

Work it by hand · the CLT on three dice-like problems
  • 100 coin flips, \(P(\ge 60\) heads\()\): se of \(\hat p\) = 0.05; \((0.6 - 0.5)/0.05 = 2\); \(P(Z \ge 2) = 0.023\). Exact: 0.028.
  • AoS Ex 5.9: Poisson(5) errors in 125 programs; \(P(\bar X < 5.5) \approx \Phi(\sqrt{125/5}\times 0.5) = \Phi(2.5) = 0.994\).
  • Sum of 12 dice: mean 42, variance \(12 \times 35/12 = 35\), SD 5.92. \(P(\text{sum} \ge 50) \approx P(Z \ge 8/5.92 = 1.35) = 0.088\); with the continuity correction (use 49.5) it is 0.102, against an exact 0.104.

How large is large enough?

There is no universal \(n\). Berry–Esseen says the error scales with the third absolute moment over \(\sigma^3\), which is large for skewed or heavy-tailed data. Use the widget: uniform data look normal at \(n = 5\); Bernoulli(0.1) is still lumpy and skewed at \(n = 30\); car prices need around 100; Cauchy never converges, because there is no \(\sigma\) to standardize by.

The delta method

Pushing a wobble through a function

The estimate \(\bar X\) wobbles around \(\mu\) with SE \(\sigma/\sqrt n\) (shaded on the x-axis). The function maps that wobble onto the y-axis (shaded there). The dashed tangent line is the delta method's approximation; the readout compares it with a simulation.

Formal · AoS Thm 5.13, Thm 9.24

If \(\sqrt n(Y_n - \mu)/\sigma \rightsquigarrow N(0,1)\) and \(g'(\mu) \ne 0\), then \(g(Y_n) \approx N\big(g(\mu), g'(\mu)^2\sigma^2/n\big)\). In practice \(\widehat{\text{se}}(g(\hat\theta)) = |g'(\hat\theta)|\,\widehat{\text{se}}(\hat\theta)\).

Then set the widget to \(g = x^2\), \(\mu = 5\), SE = 1: the simulation agrees with 10 to within a few percent. Raise SE to 2 and it doesn't: the tangent line is a bad stand-in for the curve over that wide a range.

Work it by hand · log-odds (AoS Ex 9.25)

\(\hat p = 0.6\), \(n = 100\). \(\hat\psi = \log(0.6/0.4) = 0.405\). \(g'(p) = 1/(p(1-p)) = 1/0.24 = 4.17\); \(\widehat{\text{se}}(\hat p) = \sqrt{0.24/100} = 0.049\). So \(\widehat{\text{se}}(\hat\psi) = 4.17 \times 0.049 = 0.204 = 1/\sqrt{n\hat p(1-\hat p)}\). A 95% interval for the log-odds is \(0.405 \pm 0.40 = (0.005, 0.805)\); exponentiate for the odds: \((1.01, 2.24)\). That's how logistic-regression intervals for odds ratios are made.

Questions

Think about itIn the CLT widget with car prices, does the histogram of standardized means at \(n = 200\) mean that car prices are now normal?

No. The population is unchanged; it's the distribution of averages of 200 cars that's close to normal. A single car's price is as skewed as ever.

Think about itWhy does checking "divide by s" make small-\(n\) histograms wider in the tails?

Because \(s\) is itself random. Samples where \(s\) comes out small produce large \(|Z|\). That's the effect the t-distribution corrects (page 4). With skewed data the effect is lopsided: low means come with low \(s\), so the left tail stretches more.

Check your understandingThe delta method needs \(g'(\mu) \ne 0\). What goes wrong for \(g(x) = x^2\) at \(\mu = 0\)?

The tangent is flat, so the first-order approximation says the SE is 0, which is false. The wobble of \(\bar X^2\) near 0 is second order: \(n\bar X^2/\sigma^2 \rightsquigarrow \chi^2_1\), not a normal. Set \(\mu\) to the slider's minimum with \(g = x^2\) and a large SE to see the approximation fail.
