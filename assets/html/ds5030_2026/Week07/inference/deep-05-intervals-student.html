What the 95% belongs to

A confidence interval is a pair of statistics \((a(X), b(X))\) chosen so that \(P_\theta(a \le \theta \le b) \ge 1 - \alpha\) for every \(\theta\) (AoS (6.9)). The probability is over the data, with \(\theta\) held fixed. Once you've computed \((6.92, 10.26)\), nothing random is left: the interval contains \(\mu\) or it doesn't.

Analogy · ring toss

The peg is bolted down. Your throwing arm lands the ring around the peg 95% of the time. After a throw you can see the ring, but not the peg, so you can't tell whether this throw was one of the 95%. The 95% describes the arm.

Wasserman's reading (AoS §6.3.2): over a career of unrelated studies, each with its own 95% interval, 95% of the intervals trap their own true values. CASI prefers hypothetical repetitions of this problem. Both are statements about the procedure.

Coverage you can count

Work it by hand · coverage on {1, 3, 5}, samples of 2

The nine sample means are 1, 2, 3, 2, 3, 4, 3, 4, 5 and \(\mu = 3\). The rule "\(\bar x \pm h\)" covers whenever \(|\bar x - 3| \le h\).

0.5\(\bar x = 3\): 3 samples33%

No interval rule here hits exactly 95%: with discrete data, coverage jumps. That's why exact intervals for counts are conservative (coverage "at least" \(1-\alpha\)).

Work it by hand · Berger–Wolpert (AoS Ex 6.14)

Two noisy readings \(Y_i = \theta \pm 1\) (each sign with probability 1/2). Report \(\{Y_1 - 1\}\) if the readings agree, \(\{(Y_1 + Y_2)/2\}\) if they differ. Four equally likely sign patterns: \((+,+)\) gives \(\theta\) ✓; \((-,-)\) gives \(\theta - 2\) ✗; \((+,-)\) and \((-,+)\) give \(\theta\) ✓. Coverage 3/4. Now observe 15 and 17: the readings differ, so the report is \(\{16\}\), and \(\theta\) must be 16. A "75% interval" that is certainly right. Coverage is about the rule, not the realized interval.

Normal-based intervals, step by step

Formal · AoS Thm 6.16

If \(\hat\theta \approx N(\theta, \widehat{\text{se}}^2)\), then \(\hat\theta \pm z_{\alpha/2}\widehat{\text{se}}\) has coverage \(\to 1 - \alpha\). Proof: \(P(|\hat\theta - \theta|/\widehat{\text{se}} < z_{\alpha/2}) \to P(|Z| < z_{\alpha/2}) = 1 - \alpha\).

Work it by hand · how big a sample for a ±3-point poll?

Worst case \(p = 0.5\): half-width \(1.96\sqrt{0.25/n} = 0.03\) gives \(\sqrt n = 1.96 \times 0.5/0.03 = 32.7\), so \(n \approx 1{,}068\). Halving the margin to ±1.5 points needs four times as many: about 4,270.

Bootstrap intervals

Normal, percentile, and pivotal intervals from the same replicates

Bootstrap replicates of the statistic on the car prices (or a subsample). The three intervals are drawn under the histogram. With a skewed bootstrap distribution, the percentile interval leans with the tail and the pivotal interval leans against it.

Work it by hand · three intervals from ten replicates

\(\hat\theta = 5\); replicates 3.6, 4.0, 4.4, 4.6, 4.8, 5.0, 5.2, 5.6, 6.2, 7.0 (SD 1.02); take the 10% and 90% quantiles as 4.0 and 6.2. 80% intervals: normal \(5 \pm 1.28(1.02) = (3.69, 6.31)\); percentile \((4.0, 6.2)\); pivotal \((10 - 6.2, 10 - 4.0) = (3.8, 6.0)\).

Story proof · the pivotal flip

The bootstrap world's miss \(\hat\theta^* - \hat\theta\) imitates the real world's miss \(\hat\theta - \theta\). If replicates often overshoot \(\hat\theta\) by a lot (long right tail), then \(\hat\theta\) probably overshoots \(\theta\) by a lot, so \(\theta\) sits further left. The interval stretches left: \(\hat\theta - (\theta^*_{.975} - \hat\theta) = 2\hat\theta - \theta^*_{.975}\).

BC and BCa by hand

CASI Ch 11's ladder: standard → percentile (handles skew via a hidden transformation) → BC (also median bias, via \(z_0\)) → BCa (also SE that changes with \(\theta\), via the acceleration \(a\)). Standard intervals have coverage error of order \(1/\sqrt n\); BCa of order \(1/n\).

Work it by hand · BC percentiles from a normal table

\(p_0\) = share of replicates below \(\hat\theta\); \(z_0 = \Phi^{-1}(p_0)\). The BC interval uses percentiles \(\Phi(2z_0 \pm z_{\alpha/2})\) instead of \(\alpha/2\) and \(1 - \alpha/2\).

When most replicates sit above \(\hat\theta\) (\(p_0 < 0.5\)), the estimate looks biased upward relative to its bootstrap distribution, and BC slides both ends down.

Work it by hand · why symmetric intervals fail for counts (CASI Ch 11)

One Poisson count of 10. Standard: \(10 \pm 1.96\sqrt{10} = (3.8, 16.2)\). Exact: \((5.1, 17.8)\). The Poisson's spread grows with its mean, so plausible values above 10 range further than plausible values below. A square-root transformation nearly fixes it: \(\sqrt{10} \pm 1.96/2 = (2.18, 4.14)\), squared: \((4.8, 17.2)\). The percentile method does this transformation for you without being told.

Questions

Think about itIn the ring widget with car prices at \(n = 5\), coverage is well below 95% even with the t method. Which intervals miss, the ones too high or too low?

Mostly ones entirely below \(\mu\). Small samples of car prices usually miss the few expensive cars, giving a low mean and a small SE together, so the interval is short and sits too low. Increase \(n\) and the misses even out.

Think about itIf I compute a 95% interval and then a 99% interval from the same data, is the probability that μ is in the 99% one higher?

Not in the frequentist sense: each is either right or wrong. What's higher is the long-run hit rate of the 99% procedure. You pay for it with width: \(2.576/1.96 = 1.31\) times wider.

Check your understandingWhen do the normal and percentile bootstrap intervals agree?

When the bootstrap distribution is symmetric and close to normal: then its quantiles are \(\hat\theta \pm z\cdot\widehat{\text{se}}_{\text{boot}}\). In the widget, the mean of all 92 cars nearly agrees; the SD of the first 15 cars doesn't.
