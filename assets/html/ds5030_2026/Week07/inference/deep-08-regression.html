Everything on five points

A regression table is a Wald test and a normal (or t) interval for each coefficient. On five points every number can be produced by hand. Data: \(x = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5\), \(y = 1, 3, 2, 5, 4\).

On the board · coefficients

\(\bar x = \bar y = 3\). \(\sum(x - \bar x)(y - \bar y) = 4 + 0 + 0 + 2 + 2 = 8\); \(\sum(x - \bar x)^2 = 10\). Slope \(\hat\beta_1 = 0.8\); intercept \(\hat\beta_0 = 3 - 0.8 \times 3 = 0.6\).

On the board · residuals and σ̂

Fitted \(1.4, 2.2, 3.0, 3.8, 4.6\); residuals \(-0.4, 0.8, -1.0, 1.2, -0.6\) (sum 0). RSS \(= 3.6\). \(\hat\sigma^2 = 3.6/(n - 2) = 1.2\), \(\hat\sigma = 1.095\). Two degrees of freedom were spent estimating two coefficients.

On the board · the bow-tie and a prediction

SE of the fitted line at \(x\): \(\hat\sigma\sqrt{1/5 + (x - 3)^2/10}\) = 0.49 at \(x = 3\), 0.85 at \(x = 5\), 1.15 at \(x = 6\). Predicting a new \(y\) at \(x = 6\): \(\hat y = 5.4\), SE \(= \sqrt{1.15^2 + 1.2} = 1.59\), 95% prediction interval \(5.4 \pm 3.18 \times 1.59 = (0.35, 10.45)\).

The matrix version by hand

On the board · \((X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y\) $$X^\top X = \begin{pmatrix}5 & 15\\15 & 55\end{pmatrix},\ (X^\top X)^{-1} = \frac{1}{50}\begin{pmatrix}55 & -15\\-15 & 5\end{pmatrix},\ X^\top y = \begin{pmatrix}15\\53\end{pmatrix},\ \hat\beta = \frac{1}{50}\begin{pmatrix}30\\40\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0.6\\0.8\end{pmatrix}.$$

\(\hat\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1} = 1.2 \times \frac{1}{50}\begin{pmatrix}55 & -15\\-15 & 5\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1.32 & -0.36\\-0.36 & 0.12\end{pmatrix}\). SEs \(\sqrt{1.32} = 1.15\) and \(\sqrt{0.12} = 0.346\). The correlation between \(\hat\beta_0\) and \(\hat\beta_1\) is \(-0.36/\sqrt{1.32 \times 0.12} = -0.905\): if the slope came out too steep, the intercept probably came out too low. That's the pivot of the bow-tie.

On the board · the robust (sandwich) SE for the slope

\(\widehat{\text{se}}^2_{HC0} = \sum(x_i - \bar x)^2\hat\epsilon_i^2/\big(\sum(x_i - \bar x)^2\big)^2 = \frac{4(0.16) + 1(0.64) + 0 + 1(1.44) + 4(0.36)}{100} = 0.0416\), so 0.204; HC1 multiplies by \(\sqrt{n/(n-2)} = \sqrt{5/3}\): 0.263. Smaller than the classical 0.346 here, because the biggest residuals sit at low-leverage points near \(\bar x\).

Reading a real table

Worked example · Ames, Iowa (course)

2,930 house sales, 11 coefficients, df 2,919. Row: area 113.32 2.009 56.40 0.000 109.38 117.26.

  • coef: holding age, air, and style fixed, +1 sq ft ↔ +$113 sale price on average.
  • std err: classical formula, assumes the same error spread for every house.
  • t: \(113.32/2.009 = 56.4\).
  • CI: \(113.32 \pm 1.961 \times 2.009\).
  • Change units to square meters: coef \(\times 10.764 = 1{,}220\), SE \(\times 10.764 = 21.6\), \(t\) unchanged.
  • Robust SE: 6.5, three times larger (errors grow with house size). The CI becomes \((100.6, 126.0)\).

Widget: when the SE formula is wrong

Classical vs. robust SEs under a fan-shaped error

2,000 simulated datasets: \(x\) uniform on 0–10, \(y = 1 + 0.5x + \epsilon\). The error SD depends on \(x\) in one of two shapes. The readouts compare the slope's true sampling SD with the average classical and robust SEs, and each method's coverage of the true slope.

The classical formula fails worst when the noisiest points are also the high-leverage ones (far from \(\bar x\)): "both ends" drops classical coverage to about 84%, while the robust SE stays near 94%. A fan that grows steadily with \(x\) does less damage when \(x\) is spread evenly.

Questions

Ask the classIn the drag widget, move the point at \(x = 3\) far up. Why does the slope barely change, while moving the point at \(x = 5\) changes it a lot?

Leverage. The slope is \(\sum(x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)/\sum(x_i - \bar x)^2\), so a point's pull is proportional to \(x_i - \bar x\), which is 0 at \(x = 3\). Moving the middle point raises the intercept and inflates \(\hat\sigma\) (so every SE grows), but leaves the slope where it was.

Ask the classWith strength \(\gamma = 0\) in the error-shape widget, which SE is better?

Both are about right on average (set strength to 0), but the classical one is slightly less noisy and its coverage is closer to 95% at small \(n\): when its assumption holds, it uses it. The robust SE costs a little efficiency in exchange for not depending on the assumption.

Check yourselfWhy is the prediction band so much wider than the confidence band?

The confidence band covers the average \(y\) at each \(x\), whose uncertainty shrinks like \(1/\sqrt n\). The prediction band covers a single new \(y\), which adds that point's own noise \(\hat\sigma^2\). With infinite data the confidence band collapses onto the line; the prediction band stays at roughly \(\pm 1.96\sigma\).
