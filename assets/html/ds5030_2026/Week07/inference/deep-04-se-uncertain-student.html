Is the standard error itself uncertain?

How noisy an estimated SE is, what that does to intervals, and how the t-distribution fixes it.

Why the SE is random

\(\widehat{\text{se}} = s/\sqrt n\) is computed from the same \(n\) numbers as \(\bar x\). Draw a different sample and you get a different \(s\), so a different SE. CASI's first chapter notes this regress: the accuracy estimate is "itself an algorithm" with its own accuracy. In practice the SE's own uncertainty matters a lot at small \(n\) and for skewed data, and almost not at all for large, well-behaved samples.

Analogy · a rubber ruler

Measuring "how many SEs away" with an estimated SE is measuring with a ruler you also had to estimate. A steel ruler (large \(n\)) gives the same length every time. A rubber ruler (small \(n\)) stretches and shrinks from sample to sample, and every interval inherits the stretch.

How noisy is ŝe?

The sampling distribution of the standard error

5,000 samples of size \(n\). Left: histogram of \(\widehat{\text{se}}/\text{se}\) (1 means "exactly right"). Right: each sample's mean against its SE, red when the 95% interval \(\bar x \pm 1.96\,\widehat{\text{se}}\) misses \(\mu\).

Formal · standard result (normal data)

\((n-1)S^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{n-1}\), independent of \(\bar X\); \(V(S^2) = 2\sigma^4/(n-1)\). By the delta method on \(\sqrt{\cdot}\), \(\text{sd}(S) \approx \sigma/\sqrt{2(n-1)}\), so the relative noise in \(\widehat{\text{se}}\) is about \(1/\sqrt{2(n-1)}\).

Work it by hand · the delta step, written out

\(g(v) = \sqrt v\), \(g'(v) = 1/(2\sqrt v)\). At \(v = \sigma^2\): \(\text{sd}(S) \approx \frac{1}{2\sigma}\,\text{sd}(S^2) = \frac{1}{2\sigma}\cdot\sigma^2\sqrt{\frac{2}{n-1}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2(n-1)}}\). Divide by \(\sigma\) for the relative noise. Then the table: \(n = 5 \to 1/\sqrt8 = 0.35\); \(n = 10 \to 0.24\); \(n = 30 \to 0.13\); \(n = 92 \to 0.07\). For the toy data (\(n = 5\), \(\widehat{\text{se}} = 1\)), a true SE of 0.65 or 1.35 would be unsurprising.

Work it by hand · ŝe from three resamples of the toy data

Resample \(\{2, 2, 5, 8, 8\}\): \(s^2 = (9+9+0+9+9)/4 = 9\), \(\widehat{\text{se}} = 3/\sqrt5 = 1.34\). Resample \(\{4, 5, 5, 6, 8\}\): mean 5.6, \(s^2 = (2.56 + 0.36 + 0.36 + 0.16 + 5.76)/4 = 2.3\), \(\widehat{\text{se}} = 0.68\). Same population, SEs differing by a factor of two.

What a noisy SE does to Z

Story proof · the asymmetry of being wrong

If the SE comes out 30% too small, a "±1.96 SE" interval is really ±1.37 true SEs and covers 83%. If it comes out 30% too big, the interval is ±2.55 true SEs and covers 99%. Losing 12 points one way but gaining only 4 the other means a symmetric wobble in the SE produces a net loss in coverage. Equivalently, \(Z = (\bar X - \mu)/\widehat{\text{se}}\) has heavier tails than \(N(0,1)\).

Work it by hand · the coverage arithmetic

If the SE estimate is \(c\) times the truth, the interval \(\bar x \pm 1.96\,\widehat{\text{se}}\) covers with probability \(2\Phi(1.96c) - 1\).

The t-distribution

t vs. normal

The t density with \(k\) degrees of freedom (red) against \(N(0,1)\) (black). Shaded: the area beyond ±1.96, which is what a z-based 95% interval actually misses when the statistic is t-distributed.

Story proof · a normal over a random ruler

\(T = Z/\sqrt{V/k}\) with \(Z \sim N(0,1)\) and \(V \sim \chi^2_k\) independent. Conditional on \(V\), \(T\) is normal with SD \(\sqrt{k/V}\). So \(T\) is a mixture of bells of different widths, weighted by how likely each width is. Mixing narrow and wide bells gives a sharper peak and fatter tails. As \(k\) grows, \(V/k \to 1\) and every bell has width 1.

Work it by hand · three t-tests with integer data
  • One sample: \(\{2, 4, 5, 6, 8\}\), \(H_0: \mu = 3\): \(t = 2/1 = 2\), df 4, \(t_{4,.025} = 2.776\): retain (p = 0.12). The z-test would reject (p = 0.046).
  • Nine points: \(\bar x = 54\), \(s = 6\), \(H_0: \mu = 50\): \(t = 4/2 = 2\), df 8, \(t_{8,.025} = 2.306\): retain (p = 0.081).
  • Two samples: \(\{2, 4, 6\}\) vs \(\{5, 7, 9\}\): pooled \(\hat\sigma^2 = 4\), \(t = 3/1.63 = 1.84\), df 4: retain (p = 0.14).

All three have \(t\) near 2 and all three fail to reject: at these sample sizes, "2 standard errors" isn't enough evidence once you admit the SE is estimated.

Bootstrap-t

The t-interval assumes normal data. The bootstrap-t keeps the idea of dividing by an estimated SE, but learns the distribution of \(t^* = (\bar x^* - \bar x)/\widehat{\text{se}}^*\) by resampling (CASI (11.52)–(11.53)). Each resample brings its own SE, so the noise in the SE is built into \(t^*\), including its lopsidedness for skewed data.

Work it by hand · three replicates

From \(\{2, 4, 5, 6, 8\}\): \(\{2,2,5,8,8\}\) gives \(t^* = 0/1.34 = 0\); \(\{4,5,5,6,8\}\) gives \(t^* = 0.6/0.68 = 0.89\); \(\{2,4,4,5,6\}\) gives \(t^* = -0.8/0.66 = -1.21\). With thousands of these, take the 2.5% and 97.5% points \(t^{*(.025)}, t^{*(.975)}\); the interval is \(\big(\bar x - \widehat{\text{se}}\,t^{*(.975)},\ \bar x - \widehat{\text{se}}\,t^{*(.025)}\big)\). Note the flip, as with the pivotal interval.

CASI's example: AML − ALL means, Student's interval \((0.062, 0.314)\), bootstrap-t \((0.072, 0.323)\). Close, which supported the normality assumption in that case.

Questions

Think about itIn the scatter, the red (missed) points for car prices cluster at the lower left. Why there?

Lower left means a low mean and a small SE. Those are samples that happened to miss the few expensive cars: without them, both the average and the spread come out small. A large miss divided by a small SE gives a very negative \(Z\), so the interval misses on the low side. For normal data the two are independent and the red points spread evenly.

Think about itThe t-interval is wider than the z-interval. Isn't it just being cautious?

It's being accurate. For normal data, \(t_{n-1}\) is the exact distribution of the standardized mean, so the t-interval has exactly 95% coverage and the z-interval has less. Look at the race widget with normal data: t sits on the line at every \(n\).

Check your understandingFor normal data, \(\bar X\) and \(S\) are independent. Why does that matter for the t-distribution?

It's what makes \(T = Z/\sqrt{V/k}\) a ratio of independent pieces, which is the definition of the t-distribution. For skewed data \(\bar X\) and \(S\) are positively correlated, the ratio isn't t-distributed, and its tails are lopsided (the scatter widget's red cluster).
